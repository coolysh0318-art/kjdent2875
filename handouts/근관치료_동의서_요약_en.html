Root Canal Treatment Consent — Summary

This summary is for reading before you give consent. Actual consent takes place at the clinic: after your dentist explains the treatment and answers your questions, you sign the original consent form.

1

Purpose and steps of treatment

When the pulp (nerve and blood vessels) inside a tooth is inflamed or dead because of decay, a fracture or injury, the infected pulp and bacteria are removed, the root canals are disinfected and shaped, and then sealed, so you can keep your natural tooth.

Anesthesia & access→Cleaning & disinfection (several visits)→Canal filling→Core & crown

Left untreated, pain and infection recur and inflammation can spread to the root tip and surrounding bone. A crown is usually placed afterward, though in some cases it may deliberately not be.

2

Good to know during treatment

Root canals are very thin and complex, and may be curved or blocked (calcified).

  • That is why several visits are needed.
  • Depending on the situation, the method may change or additional treatment may be needed.
  • In an emergency, measures for your safety come first; if needed, we may refer you to a hospital with more advanced care.
3

Other treatment options

Extraction + replacement

Remove the tooth and replace it with an implant, bridge or denture. You lose the natural tooth, it takes separate surgery, cost and time, and neighboring teeth may need trimming.

No treatment

Pain and infection recur, an abscess or cyst may form, inflammation may spread to neighboring teeth and the jaw, and the tooth may eventually be lost.

Root canal treatment keeps your natural tooth, but success cannot be guaranteed; if it fails, retreatment, apical surgery or extraction may be needed.

4

What we ask of you

  • Until treatment is finished, do not bite hard on the tooth — a temporarily restored tooth breaks easily.
  • If the temporary filling falls out or breaks, the canal can become contaminated — contact us and come in right away.
  • Keep every appointment until treatment is complete, and do not postpone a planned crown.
  • Follow prescriptions and instructions; tell us right away about pain, swelling or other problems, or if your health or medications change.
5

Possible complications

During and after treatment

Tenderness on biting for a few days, the tooth feeling different, a sudden flare-up of severe pain and swelling, pain, bruising or numbness at the injection site, needle breakage, brief dizziness, extra anesthesia if the tooth is acutely inflamed, limited mouth opening or jaw pain, allergy to the rubber dam, disinfectants or medicines.

Tooth and root canals

An instrument may break and stay in the canal; perforation; blocked or curved canals may not be fully treated; in retreatment, old fillings or posts may be hard to remove; material beyond the root tip; damage to crowns; cracks or root fractures not detectable beforehand — retreatment, apical surgery or extraction may be needed.

Disinfectants and nearby structures

Disinfectant can irritate the mouth, skin or eyes and permanently bleach clothing; leaking beyond the root tip can cause sudden pain, swelling and bruising. Upper molars: sinus perforation or sinusitis (ENT care). Lower molars: nerve effects causing numbness of the lower lip, chin or gums, or a change in taste (temporary or permanent). Swallowing or inhaling instruments or tooth fragments.

Treatment outcome

The success rate is high but not guaranteed; if inflammation remains or returns, retreatment, surgery or extraction may be needed. A root-canal-treated tooth fractures more easily and needs a proper final restoration; even then fracture, reinfection or discoloration can occur, so regular check-ups are needed.

Rare but serious

  • Extensive tissue damage from leaked disinfectant — hospital treatment needed.
  • Subcutaneous emphysema: air used to dry the canal enters tissue and the face or neck swells (severe cases can affect the airway).
  • Spreading infection (sepsis), anaphylaxis (shock) — emergency care and hospital transfer needed.
