Dental Implant Consent — Summary

This summary is for reading before you give consent. Actual consent takes place at the clinic: after your dentist explains the treatment and answers your questions, you sign the original consent form.

1

Purpose and steps of treatment

An artificial root is placed in the jawbone where a tooth is missing. Once it has fused with the bone, a crown is attached to restore chewing, speech and appearance.

1st surgery→2nd surgery (if needed)→Crown placement

If bone or gum is insufficient, bone grafting, a sinus lift or gum grafting may be done at the same time or separately, which can change the steps and timeline.

2

The plan may change during surgery

The amount and quality of bone and the condition of the gums can only be fully assessed during surgery.

  • The number and position of implants, and whether and how extensively bone grafting or a sinus lift is done, may be changed or added.
  • Under sedation it is difficult to ask for consent again, so you consent in advance to reasonable changes within the same treatment goal.
  • Your dentist will explain what was changed and why after surgery. If the goal or method changes substantially, separate consent will be obtained.
  • In an emergency, measures needed for your safety come first.
3

Other treatment options

Bridge

Pros shorter treatment time
Cons neighboring teeth must be trimmed

Removable denture

Pros may cost less
Cons weaker chewing and retention, feels bulky

No treatment

Neighboring teeth may tilt, the opposing tooth may drift, and the jawbone may shrink.

5

Possible complications

During and after surgery

Pain, swelling, bleeding, bruising, infection or inflammation, opening of stitches, damage to a neighboring tooth root (may need a root canal or extraction), limited mouth opening or jaw joint discomfort, side effects of anesthesia and prescribed medicines, swallowing or inhaling small parts.

Nerve injury

Numbness or altered sensation of the lower lip, chin, tongue or gums (temporary or permanent), change in taste.

Upper jaw, bone and gum grafts

Sinus perforation or sinusitis (may need an ENT specialist); rarely, the implant moves into the sinus; exposure, infection or resorption of graft material and membrane; pain or bleeding at the gum donor site (palate); loss of the graft.

Failure and long-term complications

Implant fails to fuse with bone (removal and re-placement, additional bone graft), peri-implantitis, loose or broken screws, broken or dislodged crown, implant fracture, food trapping, bite adjustment, gum recession or visible metal, changes in speech and a foreign-body feeling.

Rare but serious

  • Bleeding that is hard to stop or starts again days later (more likely with blood thinners).
  • Stopping blood thinners on your own raises the risk of stroke and heart attack. Whether to stop is decided with the doctor who prescribed them.
  • With a history of osteoporosis medication, rarely, jawbone necrosis (MRONJ).
  • Lower jaw fracture, bleeding in the floor of the mouth, anaphylaxis (shock) — emergency care and hospital transfer needed.
6

What we ask of you

  • Do not smoke — smoking raises the risk of failure. Follow prescriptions and your dentist's instructions.
  • Come for regular check-ups and keep your mouth clean with a toothbrush and interdental brushes.
  • Tell us right away about pain, swelling, bleeding or other problems, or if your health or medications change.

By signing the consent form, you confirm that you received and understood the explanation, received the “Implant Surgery Guide”, and chose implant treatment of your own free will.

The attending dentist gives the explanation and performs the main part of the surgery; if needed, other dentists at the clinic may take part in some steps. If the patient is a minor or cannot understand the document, a representative signs.
